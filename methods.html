Evidence type labels

Every metric on this site carries one of six labels describing what kind of evidence supports it. The label appears as a small chip beside or below the number.

Observed

Raw telemetry recorded directly by an IoT device or sensor in the field, transmitted to SICCA with a timestamp. No inference or extrapolation.

Official

A figure from a dated government document, survey, or gazette (e.g. Census population, WRD design note). Source and date are cited.

Estimated

Calculated from observed inputs using an explicit formula and one or more stated assumptions. The result may differ from actual physical quantities.

Modelled

Output of a calibrated numerical model or algorithm (e.g. satellite-derived water area using NDWI). Requires peer review before display.

Unavailable

The data source exists but has not been collected, linked, or validated for this site. The metric slot shows a dash or "—" until ready.

Demo

Synthetic or illustrative value generated during system design. Never displayed on the public site. Used only in internal test screens.

Water Equity Index

Not calculable

A meaningful Water Equity Index requires two inputs that are not currently available for Rajegaon:

  1. Confirmed schedule adherence — which zones receive water on which days, and whether that schedule is equitable across the village.
  2. Calibrated zone-level flows — actual volume delivered per zone, not an estimate derived from an unverified flow assumption.

Without these, any equity score would be dominated by the uncertainty in the flow assumption (A-001). Zones with longer valve-open times appear to receive more water, but this could reflect operating schedule differences rather than genuine supply inequity — or vice versa.

The dashboard displays this metric as NOT CALCULABLE — reliability data missing. No composite score is fabricated as a placeholder. The metric will appear when calibrated flow data and confirmed schedule data are available and reviewed.

Candidate formulaCoefficient of variation (CV) of zone LPCD values — lower CV = more equitable distribution. Not displayed until flow data is calibrated.

Bathymetric ground-truthing — why DSOC AI data is uniquely accurate

Estimating how much water a reservoir holds is harder than it looks. This section explains the scientific limitation of common remote-sensing approaches, and why the acoustic bathymetric survey capability that DSOC AI brings to the Jalna pilot produces defensibly accurate volume estimates that no satellite or standard aerial survey alone can match.

Why satellite imagery cannot give reservoir volume

Multispectral satellites (Sentinel-2, PlanetScope, Landsat) detect reflected light. Water absorbs near-infrared strongly, so a Normalised Difference Water Index (NDWI) extraction reliably maps the surface area of a waterbody on a given date. It cannot see below the surface. Area is two-dimensional; volume is three-dimensional.

Converting area to volume requires knowing how steeply and uniformly the reservoir bed slopes at every point — the bathymetry. Two reservoirs with identical satellite-derived surface areas can hold vastly different volumes if one is a shallow saucer and the other is a deep bowl. Without a measured depth profile, any volume figure derived from satellite area alone is speculation, not science.

What satellites give Surface water area (m²) — OBSERVED on the date of the scene
What satellites cannot give Water depth at any point; bed topography below the water line; reservoir volume
Uncertainty without bathymetry Volume error can exceed 50–200% depending on reservoir geometry — errors too large to base infrastructure decisions on

Why topographic LiDAR cannot substitute

Airborne LiDAR (Light Detection And Ranging) fires laser pulses at the ground and records the return time to build a high-resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM). This produces excellent above-water terrain data. However, laser pulses do not penetrate more than a few centimetres into turbid water.

For a reservoir that is partially or fully inundated — the normal operating condition of a storage reservoir — topographic LiDAR cannot recover submerged bathymetry. The water surface returns a reflection; the bed below it is invisible to the sensor. Only areas that happen to be exposed (dry margins at very low fill) contribute useful elevation data, and those margins are precisely where bed geometry matters least for volume calculation.

LiDAR strength Above-water terrain — excellent DEM for watershed delineation, catchment area, spillway elevation
LiDAR limit Cannot penetrate turbid water; submerged bed is a data gap in all standard LiDAR products
Consequence Volume curves derived from LiDAR alone are extrapolated from exposed margins and are unreliable for operational storage estimation

DSOC AI acoustic bathymetric survey — the ground-truth layer

Observed

DSOC AI's field methodology combines acoustic depth-sounding (sonar) with differential GNSS positioning to map the actual bed of each reservoir. A transducer emits sound pulses downward through the water column; the return echo timing gives water depth at each survey point with centimetre-level accuracy. The vessel track covers the reservoir in systematic cross-hatch or parallel lines, producing a dense point cloud of (x, y, depth) measurements across the entire inundated area.

These depth measurements — combined with a concurrent water-surface elevation from a differential GNSS reference — are interpolated into a full stage-area-storage curve: a precise mathematical relationship between water level (stage, in metres), surface area (ha), and stored volume (ML) for that specific reservoir.

This curve is the foundation of every defensible volume estimate. Once surveyed, any future water level reading — from an IoT pressure transducer, a float sensor, or a satellite-derived surface elevation — can be converted to volume with quantified accuracy rather than an assumed geometry.

Survey instrument Acoustic depth sounder (sonar) + differential GNSS — sub-decimetre depth accuracy
Output Full bed elevation model for the inundated area; validated stage-area-storage curve S(h)
Integration DEM and satellite-derived products (LiDAR, NDWI area) are calibrated against and combined with the acoustic survey — each data source fills the gap the others cannot
Volume accuracy With a surveyed stage-storage curve, volume error is dominated by the water-level sensor, typically ±1–3% — far smaller than the ±50–200% error from an unsurveyed geometry assumption
Status at Mandala Talab Acoustic bathymetric survey planned. Volume and fill metrics will be published once the survey is complete and the stage-storage curve is validated. No volume figure will be displayed before this survey is done.

Why only DSOC AI can provide this

Acoustic bathymetric survey of rural small reservoirs is technically demanding and operationally intensive. It requires specialised sonar equipment, differential GNSS base-station infrastructure, a survey vessel suitable for shallow and variable-depth conditions, field staff trained in acoustic data collection, and post-processing software to convert raw echoes into a georeferenced depth model and calibrated stage-storage curve.

Standard remote-sensing services, government survey agencies, and general GIS contractors do not routinely carry out submerged bathymetric surveys of this class at the scale of individual village-level tanks and reservoirs. Satellite-only analyses — the most common alternative — produce the area-based figures discussed above, with the volume limitations this section documents.

DSOC AI's combination of acoustic field survey, IoT instrumentation, and satellite time-series analysis produces the only technically defensible storage and fill estimates for reservoirs of this type in the Jalna district. When this site eventually publishes a volume figure for Mandala Talab, it will be grounded in a measured stage-storage curve — not an assumed geometry, a generic DEM, or an extrapolated satellite relationship.
